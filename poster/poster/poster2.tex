\begin{pbox}{navy}{panelgrey}{The problem}
  \justifying
  In chemistry, substance identity is exact: structure-based identifiers
  (\textbf{InChI\,/\,InChIKey}) link a molecule unambiguously across PubChem,
  ChEBI and beyond.\\[6mm]
  In EU regulation (\textbf{REACH}, \textbf{CLP}), a substance may be a single
  molecule, a mixture or \textbf{UVCB}, a \textbf{group} (e.g.\ PFAS, with no
  explicit set membership), or an analytical sum parameter --- identified only
  by \textbf{CAS numbers} and textual names that carry no structure.
\end{pbox}

\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{../images/mismatch.pdf}
\figcap{Fig.~1 --- Structure-based chemical identity vs.\ heterogeneous
regulatory substance types.}

\vfill
\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{../images/regulatory_groups.pdf}
\figcap{Fig.~2 --- PFAS as a regulatory group without set-membership semantics;
Sum PFOS\,+\,PFBA a derived analytical parameter.}

\vfill
\begin{pbox}{teal}{panelblue}{Research questions}
  \textbf{RQ1}\enspace To what extent can regulatory substances be linked to a
  unique chemical structure?\\[5mm]
  \textbf{RQ2}\enspace How reliable are CAS numbers as cross-dataset
  identifiers?\\[5mm]
  \textbf{RQ3}\enspace Can embedding-based or LLM methods compensate for absent
  structure identifiers?
\end{pbox}

\begin{callout}{voyellow}{vodark}
  {\fontsize{100}{104}\selectfont\bfseries\color{vodark}37.5\%}\\[6mm]
  {\fontsize{31}{38}\selectfont\bfseries\color{vodark}
   of regulatory substance entries have no machine-readable structural identity.}
\end{callout}

% ==================================================================== COLUMN 2
\begin{column}{0.315\textwidth}
\begin{minipage}[t][\colh][s]{\linewidth}
\strut\par

\begin{pbox}{navy}{panelgrey}{Approach}
  \justifying
  We integrated \textbf{18 ECHA-based datasets} (41,813 records): obligation and
  activity lists, REACH registrations, the EU Pesticides Database, OSPAR and a
  Flemish administrative list. Each entry was linked to InChIKey via PubChem and
  assigned to a \textbf{seven-tier linkability taxonomy}.\\[6mm]
  Substance names were matched to ${\sim}$4,800 ChemOnt classes using four
  sentence-transformer models and Claude Sonnet 4.6, benchmarked against 28,204
  ClassyFire assignments.
\end{pbox}

\begin{minipage}[t]{0.315\linewidth}\stat{green}{62.5\%}{link to a defined structure (tier 1)}\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.315\linewidth}\stat{statred}{37.5\%}{non-structure-based entries}\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.315\linewidth}\stat{navy}{39.2\%}{best LLM Hit@1 (still a minority)}\end{minipage}

\vfill
\begin{pbox}{navy}{panelblue}{Linkability\ \ (RQ1)}
  \justifying
  Of 33,452 unique entries, \textbf{62.5\% (20,909)} link directly via InChIKey.
  The other \textbf{37.5\%} resist structure-based linking: 11.6\% unresolved CAS
  (tier 4), 10.8\% mixtures or UVCBs (tier 5), and 14.9\% lack any usable
  identifier (tier 7).
\end{pbox}

\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{../images/Analysis_4bd_v_Entity_type_and_linkability_vertical.pdf}
\figcap{Fig.~3 --- Entity-type composition (A) and seven-tier linkability (B)
across 18 sources.}

\vfill
\begin{pbox}{navy}{panelgrey}{CAS unreliability\ \ (RQ2)}
  \justifying
  \textbf{70.0\%} of structure-resolved InChIKeys appear in only one source ---
  CAS is insufficient as a shared key.\\[6mm]
  Ambiguity compounds this: 195 InChIKeys map to multiple CAS numbers (up to
  seven); 4 CAS numbers resolve to two distinct InChIKeys (e.g.\ toluene
  diisocyanate $\rightarrow$ two positional isomers).
\end{pbox}

\begin{callout}{vodark}{voyellow}
  {\fontsize{42}{52}\selectfont\bfseries\color{white}
   CAS numbers introduce ambiguity rather than resolving it.}
\end{callout}

\end{minipage}

\strut\par

\begin{pbox}{statred}{panelgrey}{Semantic methods fail\ \ (RQ3)}
  \justifying
  Embedding-based matching collapses: the best model reaches
  \textbf{Hit@1 = 5.75\%}; all others score below 1\%. Claude Sonnet 4.6 reaches
  \textbf{39.2\%} --- a seven-fold gain, but it still misclassifies the majority.
  Text cannot substitute for structure.
\end{pbox}

\vfill
\begin{pbox}{green}{panelblue}{What works: the knowledge graph\ \ (C4, C5)}
  \justifying
  ${\sim}$17,500 regulatory substances (SKOS concepts) are integrated with
  structure-based identity, ChemOnt classification and ChEBI biological roles ---
  \textbf{${\sim}$46,600 cross-domain links}, enabling composite priority scoring
  that is infeasible on fragmented data.\\[6mm]
  LLM-based scope mapping yields \textbf{304 validated SKOS triples}
  (285 broadMatch, 18 exactMatch, 1 narrowMatch) as a legislative--ChemOnt
  crosswalk.
\end{pbox}

\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{../images/knowledge_graph.pdf}
\figcap{Fig.~4 --- Integrated knowledge graph linking identity, classification
and biological roles.}

\vfill
\begin{pbox}{navydeep}{panelgold}{Conclusions \& recommendations}
  \justifying
  Over one-third of entries cannot be linked to a structure; CAS adds ambiguity;
  neither embeddings nor LLMs can replace structural information. Manual
  reconciliation (${\sim}$3,150 person-years for REACH) is infeasible.
  \textbf{Harmonisation must happen at the source, not downstream.}\\[6mm]
  \textbf{1}\enspace Require structure-based identifiers (InChIKey; MInChI for
  mixtures) across regulatory instruments.\\[4mm]
  \textbf{2}\enspace Mandate explicit substance-group representation using
  formal ontologies such as ChemOnt.\\[4mm]
  \textbf{3}\enspace Publish regulatory data as linked data with persistent URIs
  and SKOS links (FAIR / Interoperable Europe Act).
\end{pbox}
